What I Do

Organizations create knowledge every day, and the company's expertise develops through its people's experience. Yet so much of that knowledge remains surprisingly fragile across entire industries.

My work focuses on preserving that knowledge when people leave, responsibilities change, or teams lose track of why a decision was made.

I create documentation, knowledge bases, process guides, reference materials, and training resources that help organizations retain context and transfer expertise. My knowledge management work focuses on how organizations capture, organize, and sustain what they know. Through documentation engineering, I design the structures, standards, and processes that make that knowledge usable and maintainable.

When I build a knowledge base, I consider what readers need to do, what they already know, and how they'll find their way through the information. I design its architecture, information hierarchy, and navigation around those journeys, then establish writing conventions, image markup, and presentation standards so the content works together.

I also design how maintenance should work, including who owns content, how it's reviewed, and when it needs updating, so teams can sustain the system over time.

How I Got Here

My experience spans SaaS organizations and operational work in industries including fintech, supply chain, cybersecurity, and entertainment.

Across those environments, I noticed the same pattern repeatedly. Teams solved problems and developed expertise, but a lot of that understanding was never captured anywhere people could find it later. When people left, priorities shifted, or time passed, important context disappeared with them.

Over time, I've become increasingly interested in the challenge behind the challenge: ensuring the knowledge needed to solve recurring problems remains available in the future.

That pattern shaped the direction of my career, drawing me deeper into documentation, knowledge management, and organizational learning.

My Approach

Preserve nuance. Good documentation should retain the context, assumptions, and implications that make knowledge useful. A document can be technically accurate while still failing to communicate what matters.

Exercise judgment. Preserving knowledge doesn't mean recording everything. The most valuable documentation balances completeness with usability, helping people find and understand what they need without overwhelming them.

Support action. Documentation succeeds when it enables something: faster onboarding, better decisions, fewer repeated mistakes, stronger collaboration, or more consistent execution. Knowledge becomes valuable when people can use it.
